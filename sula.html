Sula Fences The Great Gatsby Cross-Text Themes
Toni Morrison · 1973

Sula

The Bottom, Medallion, Ohio · 1919–1965

174 pages National Book Award finalist Pittsburgh Cycle adjacent
Context & Setting
Place
The Bottom
Founded on a lie
A Black neighborhood in the hills above Medallion, Ohio — named with a dark joke. A white farmer tricked a freed slave into accepting hillside land by calling it "the bottom of heaven." This founding deception runs through everything: Black life in Medallion is built on stolen ground. By the novel's end the Bottom is demolished for a golf course — erased by the same white world that created it.
Time
WWI to 1965
History shaping private lives
The novel spans fifty years. WWI traumatizes Shadrack; the Great Migration pulls Pearl north; Jim Crow shapes every economic decision. The New River Road bridge Jude can't get hired to build is Morrison's shorthand for how national racism blocks private lives. 1965 — one year after the Civil Rights Act — is when the Bottom disappears. Timing is never accidental in Morrison.
Family Tree — The Peace Family
Generation 1 · Matriarch
Eva Peace
Abandoned by BoyBoy · Runs a boarding house · One-legged
The terrifying gravitational center of the Peace family. Abandoned by her husband BoyBoy with three children and no money, Eva almost certainly sacrificed her leg under a train for the insurance payout — then returned 18 months later with enough to build a house and never discussed it again. She rules her household with absolute, possessive love: she burns her son Plum alive to end his suffering, and watches Hannah burn in the yard without moving. Eva's love is real and devastating in equal measure. She later accuses Sula of having the same cold, watching eye — and she's not wrong.
"You don't love nobody but yourself." — Eva to Sula
Generation 2 · Eldest daughter
Pearl Peace
Marries at 14 and moves to Flint, Michigan. Largely disappears from the narrative — Morrison gives her almost no interiority. Her early exit through conventional marriage represents one escape route from the Bottom, but also an erasure. Women who choose conformity early are swallowed by it.
Generation 2 · Middle daughter
Hannah Peace
Sula's mother · Sensual, free-spirited
Warm, alive, and completely free with men — she sleeps with neighbors' husbands without guilt or attachment. Unlike Sula, the community tolerates her sexuality because she asks nothing of the men; she's non-threatening. She loves Sula genuinely, but once tells a friend she doesn't like her — overheard by young Sula, a wound that never closes. Dies horribly when her dress catches fire in the backyard. Eva watches from an upstairs window without moving to save her.
"She was an easy woman... she didn't need anything from you."
Generation 2 · Son
Plum (Ralph) Peace
Eva's most beloved · WWI veteran · Heroin addict
Eva's youngest and most cherished. Returns from WWI destroyed, descends into heroin addiction, regressing into helpless infancy. Eva burns him alive in his sleep — she frames it as mercy: she would rather kill him than watch him crawl back into her womb. One of the novel's most disturbing scenes and one of its most honest: Eva's love has always been about control, not freedom. She decides when and how people she loves get to exist.
"I held him close first. For a long time." — Eva, on killing Plum
Generation 3 · Protagonist
Sula Peace
Hannah's daughter · Birthmark over one eye · Returns after 10 years away
The novel's fierce, uncompromising center. Born with a large birthmark over one eye — the community reads it as a rose, a snake, Hannah's ashes, evil itself. Morrison uses the birthmark to show how a community projects its fears onto a woman who refuses definition. Sula witnesses Eva's violence and absorbs it; she absorbs Hannah's sensuality too. At 12, she watches Chicken Little drown and says nothing. At 17, she cuts her own fingertip to warn boys not to mess with her. She leaves at 18, returns at 27 utterly herself — sleeps with Nel's husband Jude almost carelessly, puts Eva in a nursing home, refuses every role available to her. The community calls her evil. Morrison asks: by whose standard, decided by whom?
"I got my mind. And what goes on in it. Which is to say, I got me." — Sula
Family Tree — The Wright Family
Background
Helene was born to Rochelle — a Creole prostitute in New Orleans — and raised rigidly by her grandmother Cecile Sabat. She reinvented herself completely, becoming the most proper woman in the Bottom. Her entire identity is a flight from her origins. Everything about her — the starched house, the perfect manners — is an argument against where she came from.
Generation 1 · Matriarch
Helene Wright née Sabat
Obsessively respectable · Moral opposite of Eva
Where Eva is raw, Helene is polished. Where Eva destroys from love, Helene suffocates from decorum. She raises Nel with such rigid expectations of propriety that Nel enters adulthood with almost no independent self. The novel's pivotal Helene scene: on a train south, a white conductor humiliates her and she responds with a submissive smile. The Black soldiers in the car look away in shame. Nel watches and silently resolves not to become her mother — then does exactly that.
Wiley Wright
Helene's husband · Railroad worker · Largely absent
Works the railroad; rarely present. His absence is part of Morrison's argument: the men in these women's lives are often peripheral to the real drama. Helene runs the house in his name; his authority is invoked but never embodied.
Generation 2 · Co-protagonist
Nel Wright
Sula's best friend · Marries Jude Greene · Stays in Medallion
Everything Sula is not — and Morrison insists this is not a hierarchy. Where Sula chooses radical selfhood, Nel chooses community. She marries Jude, keeps house, stays in the Bottom, raises children. For decades she believes she is the moral one. When Jude leaves for Sula, she grieves — but never examines her own guilt (she too watched Chicken Little drown without speaking). Near the end, after years of thinking she grieved for Jude, she realizes: she has been grieving Sula all along. The novel's final line — "girl, girl, girlgirlgirl" — belongs to her. It is Morrison's most devastating sentence.
"It was a fine cry — loud and long — but it had no bottom and it had no top, just circles and circles of sorrow."
Other Key Characters
WWI Veteran · Community figure
Shadrack
Shell-shocked by war, he returns unable to control his own hands and terrified of random death. His response: found National Suicide Day (January 3rd) — a day to face death collectively so it loses power the rest of the year. The community fears him, then absorbs the ritual without understanding it. He and Sula share a strange wordless bond. After her death, he leads the Final National Suicide Day crowd — they fall into a flooded tunnel and drown. He survives, alone, still ringing his bell.
"If you organized it, if you made it a day, then it lose its teeth."
Nel's husband · Catalyst
Jude Greene
Marries Nel not out of love but because he needs a witness to his pain — someone whose need for him shores up an identity damaged by racism. He can't get hired to build the New River Road bridge; marriage becomes his consolation. His affair with Sula is almost incidental. He leaves Nel and vanishes. Morrison makes him pathetic rather than villainous: his real function is to reveal what Nel built herself on.
Sula's one serious lover
Ajax (Albert Jacks)
The only man who genuinely interests Sula because he is truly free — rootless, interesting, treats her as an equal. When Sula begins showing signs of possessiveness (she learns his real name; she cleans the house), Ajax recognizes the closing trap of domesticity and leaves without a word. He is what Sula values and cannot hold without destroying through attachment.
Young boy · The central crime
Chicken Little
Accidentally drowned when Sula swings him by his hands at the river and he slips. Both Sula and Nel watch him sink and say nothing. The shared guilt bonds them; the shared silence condemns them both equally. Morrison plants this so that decades later, when Nel finally confronts it, we understand: the "good" one was as guilty as the "evil" one. The crime is shared. Only one wears the label.
Eva's three boarders
The Deweys
Three unrelated boys Eva takes in and names Dewey — all of them. They merge into one entity, indistinguishable even to each other, remaining childlike and stunted their whole lives. Morrison uses them to explore how naming and expectation can collapse individuality entirely. They are also darkly funny, which is rare in this novel.
Eva's husband · Absent wound
BoyBoy
Leaves Eva with three children and no money, then returns years later prosperous — with another woman — as if to taunt her. Eva hates him calmly and forever. His abandonment is the original wound of the Peace family: it forces Eva to destroy her own body to survive, and that act of survival becomes the source of her authority and her violence. Everything flows from this man who barely appears.
Major Themes
Good vs. Evil — Who Decides?
Morrison's central provocation: the community calls Sula evil, but Nel also watched Chicken Little drown. Eva kills her son and is called a mother; Sula puts Eva in a nursing home and is called a monster. The novel argues that "good" and "evil" are social designations, not moral facts — assigned to women based on how well they serve community needs. Sula is not evil. She is inconvenient.
Female Selfhood & Freedom
Sula is Morrison's portrait of a Black woman who refuses every available role — wife, mother, respectable neighbor — and creates herself from within. The tragedy isn't that she fails. It's that there is no social form for such a self to inhabit. Her freedom has nowhere to live. She can only be read as destruction by a community that has no language for a woman who simply exists for herself.
Community as Double-Edged
The Bottom needs Sula to function. Her presence makes neighbors love each other more, guard their husbands, feel morally superior. When she dies, they lose their scapegoat and their cohesion. The tunnel disaster — Shadrack's final National Suicide Day crowd falling into a flooded excavation — is their self-destruction without her to hold them together. Community here is both shelter and trap, and often the trap is invisible.
Friendship as the Central Relationship
Morrison places the Sula–Nel bond above every romance in the novel. They are each other's mirrors and completions — less two people than two halves of a whole. Nel's revelation at the end, that she grieved Sula all along and not Jude, is the novel's thesis: this friendship was the most important thing either of them ever had, and neither understood it until it was over.
Race, Place & Erasure
The Bottom's founding (a trick on a freed slave) and destruction (for a golf course) frame the entire novel. Black community in America is built on stolen or tricked-out ground and always vulnerable to erasure. This isn't background — it's the argument. The Bottom disappearing is Morrison's final word on what America does with Black life once it's no longer useful to someone else.
Motherhood Without Redemption
Every mother in Sula fails on some level: Eva burns her son, Hannah admits she doesn't like her daughter, Helene suffocates Nel's identity, Sula never becomes one. Morrison refuses to let motherhood save or redeem women. It is just another role, and roles are not the same as selves. The novel is a sustained argument against the myth of redemptive motherhood.

Exam Writing Tips
If your prompt is about good vs. evil
Argue that Morrison deliberately refuses to assign good or evil cleanly. Use Chicken Little (both girls watch), Eva/Plum (murder framed as love), and the community's scapegoating of Sula to show "evil" is a social label, not a moral fact. Your thesis: the novel doesn't ask whether Sula is evil — it asks who gets to decide, and why that power belongs to the community rather than to Sula herself.
If your prompt is about identity or freedom
Contrast Sula and Nel as two responses to the same problem: how does a Black woman build a self in a world that only offers her roles? Sula chooses radical selfhood at the cost of belonging. Nel chooses belonging at the cost of selfhood. Neither survives whole. The novel's final lines suggest Nel only understands the trade-off she made after it's too late to undo it.
If your prompt is about community
The Bottom is both home and cage for its residents. Argue that Morrison shows community as a mechanism for enforcing conformity — it requires a scapegoat (Sula) to maintain its internal bonds. Track this: before Sula returns, the community is fragmented; while she's there, it unites against her; after she dies, it destroys itself. Sula's presence was the community's organizing principle, even while they hated her for it.
