August Wilson · 1985 · Pittsburgh Cycle, Play 6

Fences

Pittsburgh's Hill District · 1957–1965

Pulitzer Prize 1987 Tony Award Best Play 2 Acts · 9 Scenes
Context & Setting
Place
The Hill District, Pittsburgh
1957 — The decade after baseball integration
Set entirely in the small yard of the Maxson house in Pittsburgh's predominantly Black Hill District — vibrant community and cage at the same time. 1957 is deliberate: Jackie Robinson integrated baseball in 1947 and the major leagues are slowly, resentfully opening. Troy Maxson came a generation too early. The drama lives in that gap — what it costs a man to be exactly on the wrong side of history by a decade.
Context
August Wilson's Pittsburgh Cycle
A decade-by-decade chronicle of Black American life
Fences is the sixth of ten plays in Wilson's epic project: one play per decade of the 20th century, all set in Pittsburgh's Hill District. Wilson wanted to give Black Americans the same kind of specific, epic, particular history that white American theater had always claimed for itself. The 1950s play is about the Great Migration's second wave, postwar prosperity that excluded Black families, and the early Civil Rights movement — all filtered through one man's yard.
Characters
Protagonist · 53 years old
Troy Maxson
Garbage collector · Former Negro League baseball player
The most complex character August Wilson ever created. Troy was a genuinely gifted baseball player — possibly one of the best in the country — who played in the Negro Leagues because integration hadn't happened yet. By the time it did, he was too old. That injustice is real and devastating. But Wilson refuses to make Troy simply a victim: he cheats on Rose, drives Cory away, brings home an illegitimate child, uses Gabriel's disability check to buy the house, and prevents Cory from pursuing the football scholarship he himself could never have. Troy repeats his father's damage without recognizing it. He is enormous and flawed and tragic in the classical sense — his greatness and his destruction come from the same source: an absolute refusal to be diminished, which makes him diminish everyone around him.
"I had me a chance once. You got yours coming. The time ain't now." — Troy to Cory
Troy's wife · Moral center
Rose Maxson
The play's true moral conscience and its most devastating speaker. She has built her entire life inside the fence of her marriage, accepting Troy's limitations and protecting his ego for eighteen years. When he reveals Alberta's pregnancy, her response is one of the most powerful moments in American drama: she agrees to raise the baby (Raynell) but tells Troy he is now a womanless man. She chooses the innocent child. She closes the door on Troy completely. Her fence protects her now; his can no longer reach her.
"I been standing with you! I been right here with you, Troy. I got a life too. I gave eighteen years of my life to stand in the same spot with you." — Rose
Troy's son · 17 · Football recruit
Cory Maxson
A college recruiter is coming to scout Cory — the same door baseball opened for other men, in a different sport in a slightly more open era. Troy forbids it, ostensibly to protect Cory from disappointment, but really because he cannot bear watching his son succeed where he failed, or become something he cannot understand. The father-son dynamic is the play's engine. Troy's destruction of Cory's dream is the clearest expression of how trauma passes between generations. By the end Cory has enlisted in the Marines and returns for Troy's funeral still unreconciled, still carrying Troy's damage and his own.
"You ain't never gave me nothing! You ain't never done nothing but hold me back." — Cory
Troy's best friend · Witness
Jim Bono
Met Troy in prison; his oldest friend. Bono is the play's audience surrogate — he admires Troy, knows him completely, and watches with increasing unease as Troy's choices destroy his family. He's the one person Troy is honest with (to a point). His friendship cools after the Alberta revelation, not from judgment but because he can't watch anymore. Bono also represents a man who lives decently within limitations; his marriage to Lucille is not spectacular but it is faithful.
Troy's brother · Brain-damaged veteran
Gabriel Maxson
Returned from WWII with a metal plate in his head, believing he is the Archangel Gabriel. He carries a trumpet and believes he is preparing to open heaven's gates for Saint Peter. Troy used Gabriel's disability check to buy the family house — a moral compromise he lives with. Gabriel is the play's spiritual register. His final scene: he blows his broken trumpet (it makes no sound), then dances. The stage direction reads: the gates of heaven open. It's one of the most stunning endings in American drama.
"That's the way that go!" — Gabriel, after his dance
Troy's son from first relationship
Lyons Maxson
Troy's oldest son, a jazz musician — which Troy cannot respect because it doesn't mean security or hard work on Troy's terms. Lyons visits mainly to borrow money. Their relationship is warmer than Troy and Cory's precisely because Lyons gave up on his father's approval long ago. He appears at the end having served time in prison. He is the only person who seems genuinely sad at Troy's funeral.
Troy's daughter by Alberta
Raynell Maxson
Alberta dies in childbirth. Rose agrees to raise the baby: "She's innocent, and you can't visit the sins of the father upon the child." Raynell appears at the play's end, age 7, tending the garden Rose planted. Her duet with Cory on the old family song is the play's closing image: Troy's children, carrying his music and his damage, still finding a way to sing together. The garden Rose tends is what endures.
The Fence — Symbol Analysis
What does the fence mean?

Rose's fence: She asks Troy to build it. For Rose, the fence is love made physical — it keeps the family in, keeps the world out, protects what matters. She wants to enclose what she has before the world takes it. The fence is her deepest act of hope.

Troy's fence: Troy resists building it for most of the play. For Troy, fences are what the world builds against him — the color line that kept him out of the major leagues, the economic walls that block Black advancement. He doesn't want to build his own cage. His resistance is a refusal to accept limitation.

Bono's reading: "Some people build fences to keep people out... and other people build fences to keep people in." This is the play's thesis line. The same object means opposite things depending on who built it and why.

The irony: Troy finishes the fence the very day he confesses the affair to Rose. He builds it — finally, completely — and immediately destroys what it was meant to protect. The completed fence marks the end of the marriage. What Rose wanted it to hold, Troy has just let go.

Major Themes
The American Dream & Race
Troy is the American Dream's nightmare: a man with the talent, work ethic, and drive the dream demands — who was excluded by race. His bitterness is not weakness; it is the rational response to a rigged game. Wilson's point: the American Dream is not a universal promise. For Black Americans in 1950s Pittsburgh, it was a myth that demanded belief while systematically denying access — and then blamed men like Troy for the bitterness their exclusion produced.
Generational Trauma
Troy's father was brutal and abandoning. Troy learned that survival required hardness. He passes this to Cory — not because he doesn't love his son, but because he cannot imagine love that doesn't look like endurance and discipline. The play's devastating insight: Troy's hardness is not cruelty. It is the only survival tool he was ever given, and he gives it to Cory because it's the most valuable thing he has. It destroys Cory anyway.
Death as Constant Presence
Troy speaks of Death as a personal adversary — "I wrestled with Death for three days and three nights." He personifies it, challenges it, turns it into a wrestling match he believes he won. This is his coping mechanism: if Death is a person, you can fight it; if it's random, you can't. His actual death comes quietly, alone in the yard, doing his exercises. The gap between the epic death he imagined and the ordinary one he got is one of the play's quietest tragedies.
What a Father Owes a Son
Troy believes he owes Cory food, shelter, and the chance to work — not emotional support, not permission to dream, not love as Cory might recognize it. "A man ain't supposed to like his father." He provides for Cory in the only way he was taught to provide, without recognizing that provision and love are not the same thing. This is the play's saddest gap: Troy loves Cory completely and has no language for it that doesn't come out as control.
Loyalty & Betrayal
Alberta represents the only place Troy allows himself tenderness and escape from responsibility. Wilson doesn't excuse the infidelity — but he explains it. Troy cannot be small or weak with Rose; she holds him accountable in ways Alberta does not. His betrayal of Rose is real, devastating, and human. The tragedy is that Rose's clear-eyed moral strength — the thing that makes her the play's moral center — is also what Troy could not be vulnerable around.
Sports as Metaphor
Baseball runs through the play as missed life and borrowed language. Troy uses baseball terms constantly — "full count," "three strikes," "got to take the crookeds with the straights." Sport was the arena where America tested its myths of meritocracy; it was also one of the last to integrate. Troy calling out the integration that came too late for him is not bitterness — it's a fact. His inability to see Cory's football as opportunity mirrors his inability to see past his own wound.

Exam Writing Tips
If your prompt is about the American Dream
Argue that Wilson uses Troy to show the Dream is not race-neutral. Troy has everything it requires except access — then gets blamed for the bitterness his exclusion produces. Connect to Cory: the tragedy isn't just what happened to Troy, it's that Troy's wound becomes Cory's wall. The Dream's cruelest trick is turning its own victims into gatekeepers for the next generation.
If your prompt is about the fence symbol
Track who wants it built and why. Rose's fence = love and protection. Troy's resistance = refusal to build his own cage. The irony of Troy completing it the day he betrays Rose. Gabriel's broken trumpet at the end opening heaven's gates = the only fence that truly matters is spiritual and beyond Troy's control. Use Bono's "keep people out / keep people in" as your thesis line.
If your prompt is about fathers and sons
Don't make Troy simply a villain. Wilson is careful to show Troy loves Cory — he just has no tools for expressing it that don't look like domination. The strongest argument: Troy passes to Cory exactly what his own father passed to him (hardness, discipline, emotional unavailability) because those were his only survival tools. The cycle is not broken by Cory rejecting Troy — but it might be interrupted by Raynell's garden and the song at the end.
