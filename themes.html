Themes & Connections

Six deep themes across Sula, Fences, and The Great Gatsby — with synthesis arguments, a full comparison table, and exam prompts.

How to Use This Page
Each theme below shows how all three texts handle it, then offers a Synthesis — the deeper argument that connects them. The strongest in-class essays build one arguable claim and support it with 2–3 specific moments across texts, not just general similarities. Use the Exam Prompts at the bottom to practice thesis approaches before your exam.
Theme 1
1
The American Dream — Promise & Betrayal

All three texts engage with America's foundational promise — that hard work, talent, and desire can remake a person and unlock prosperity. All three expose the lie inside that promise. But they expose different lies for different people.

Sula — Morrison
The Dream was never extended to the Bottom's Black residents in the first place. The neighborhood is founded on a trick played on a freed slave and demolished for a golf course by 1965. Morrison doesn't mourn the Dream — she shows it was never offered. Sula doesn't pursue it at all; she opts out of every version of it. The most pointed critique: the Bottom's destruction comes not from failure to dream, but from a system designed to displace.
Fences — Wilson
Troy Maxson is the Dream's most bitter casualty: he had the talent, work ethic, and ambition — and was excluded by race from the arena where it could have paid off. By the time integration came, he was too old. His bitterness is the rational response to a rigged game. The Dream's cruelest move: the excluded become their own gatekeepers. Troy blocks Cory's opportunity, turning his own wound into the next generation's wall.
Gatsby — Fitzgerald
Gatsby achieves the Dream's material form — mansion, parties, reinvented identity — and discovers it is structurally hollow. He cannot buy into East Egg. He cannot buy Daisy's real love or the past she represents. His Dream is blocked not by race but by class: new money cannot become inherited belonging. The green light is always across the water. The final image frames America as a dream that always runs backward.
Synthesis
Fitzgerald shows the Dream is inaccessible by class; Wilson shows it is inaccessible by race; Morrison shows it was never structurally offered to Black Americans at all. Together, the three texts argue the American Dream is not a universal promise but a selective one — and the selectivity is the point. It requires people to believe they failed personally so they don't see they were excluded systematically. The Dream needs its failures to maintain its credibility for everyone else.
Theme 2
2
Parents, Children & The Inheritance of Damage

Every text is about what parents do to children — not always from cruelty, often from love, sometimes from wounds they never chose. The damage passes. The question each text asks differently: can it be stopped?

Sula — Morrison
Eva's destructive love flows into Sula's cold watching eye. Hannah's casual admission of not liking Sula becomes an unhealable wound. The Peace women love enormously and damage each generation with the same gesture, slightly mutated. Sula breaks the chain by refusing to become a mother — but that refusal means the chain ends with her death. Morrison asks: is refusing to reproduce trauma a form of freedom, or a form of extinction?
Fences — Wilson
This is the play's central engine. Troy's father was brutal and abandoning. Troy becomes hard in response, then imposes hardness on Cory as love. The fence Troy builds becomes the wall Cory must escape. The final scene — Cory and Raynell singing their grandfather's old song — suggests the inheritance isn't only damage: there is also voice, music, the capacity to continue. But it doesn't erase what Troy took from Cory.
Gatsby — Fitzgerald
Gatsby is an orphan of class — self-made and self-destroyed. Tom and Daisy's damage flows not from trauma but from privilege: they inherited carelessness. Daisy abandons Gatsby partly because she was never taught that her choices have consequences for people below her class. Their daughter (barely present) is their most chilling inheritance: born into a world her parents have already spoiled, destined to be "a beautiful little fool."
Synthesis
What parents pass to children is not primarily money or biology but a relationship to the world: Eva's daughters inherit her watching; Troy's sons inherit his hardness; Daisy's daughter inherits her mother's beautiful uselessness. Morrison and Wilson write from inside the damage — their characters are conscious of it and cannot escape it. Fitzgerald writes the damage as unconscious — the Buchanans don't know they are breaking things. This is the texts' sharpest divergence: trauma in Sula and Fences is felt and lived; in Gatsby, it is performed without awareness.
Theme 3
3
Identity, Self-Invention & The Cost of Becoming

Each text features at least one character who attempts to construct an identity from scratch — to become someone the world didn't plan for them to be. All three show this is possible, partial, and costly.

Sula — Morrison
Sula creates herself entirely from within, refusing every available role. Her freedom is genuine — but it has no social form, no community to receive it. She can only be read as deviance. Helene Wright, by contrast, reinvents herself through total conformity. Both strategies fail to produce belonging. Morrison's implication: a Black woman's self-invention in mid-century America will always be read through someone else's lens.
Fences — Wilson
Troy constructed his identity around baseball — his body, his skill, his sense of his own exceptionalism. When that arena was closed to him, he rebuilt himself as provider and survivor, but the foundation (his greatness, his missed chance) never stopped pulling at him. His identity is built on a wound. Cory is forced to rebuild from nothing after Troy destroys his football prospects — he joins the Marines, reinventing himself through the only institution that will have him.
Gatsby — Fitzgerald
James Gatz's reinvention as Jay Gatsby is the most complete self-invention in American literature — new name, new voice, new history, new wealth. But it is entirely contingent on Daisy's acceptance. Without her, the construction is just performance. When Tom exposes him as "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere," Gatsby's invented self collapses. He dies as the ghost of James Gatz, alone in his pool, waiting for a call that doesn't come.
Synthesis
All three texts show that self-invention is possible but precarious: it requires either social acceptance (Gatsby needs Daisy; Helene needs the Bottom's approval) or a radical willingness to exist without it (Sula, partially Troy). Characters who build their identities around external validation are destroyed when it's withdrawn. Characters who build from within are destroyed by isolation. Morrison's conclusion is the darkest: there is no safe architecture for a self — only degrees of exposure.
Theme 4
4
Place, Community & The Politics of Belonging

Every text is acutely geographic — where characters live, what those places mean, and who gets to belong in them is never neutral. Place is always political.

Sula — Morrison
The Bottom was given as a trick; it's vibrant despite its conditions; and it's eventually demolished for a golf course. It never truly belonged to its residents. Within it, Sula is doubly displaced — rejected by the community she was born into. When she dies, the community loses its scapegoat and its cohesion. The Bottom cannot survive without the thing it hated most.
Fences — Wilson
The Maxson yard is the entire world of the play — every scene takes place in it. It is both refuge and cage. Troy bought the house with Gabriel's disability check — a moral compromise haunting every scene. The yard is where Troy holds court, tells his stories, builds the fence, and dies. It is a Black man's small sovereignty in a city that denies him larger ones.
Gatsby — Fitzgerald
East Egg and West Egg encode belonging as birthright. Gatsby's mansion is physically larger than the Buchanans' but cannot purchase inherited legitimacy. The Valley of Ashes is the geography of exclusion — the place for people who belong nowhere else. Fitzgerald maps class onto geography with mathematical precision: you can always tell who someone is by where they live, and you cannot cross the water between them without consequence.
Synthesis
In all three texts, the places characters live are assigned — not chosen — by systems of power they cannot fully control. The Bottom was a trick; the Hill District is where Pittsburgh put its Black workers; East/West Egg encodes class inheritance. Characters who try to move between places (Gatsby toward East Egg; Sula returning after 10 years; Helene visiting New Orleans) are all exposed and diminished by the attempt. Place in these texts is not backdrop — it is a mechanism of social control that operates by making its own constraints feel natural.
Theme 5
5
Women, Expectation & Interiority

Each text has at its center women whose value to the narrative is partly defined by what they refuse or accept. The texts differ sharply in how much interiority they grant their female characters — which is itself a moral argument.

Sula — Morrison
Morrison gives her women the most interiority of any author here. Sula, Nel, Eva, Hannah are all rendered from inside. Sula's refusal of every female role is the novel's central act; Nel's acceptance of every female role is equally examined. Morrison refuses to privilege either choice — both women suffer, both have moments of freedom and loss. The problem is not individual women's choices but a social structure that makes freedom and belonging mutually exclusive for them.
Fences — Wilson
Rose is the play's moral center, but we see everything through Troy's lens until she breaks open in Act Two. Her speech after Troy's confession is the play's emotional turning point: eighteen years of standing with him, and he still treated her as backdrop. Her acceptance of Raynell — "she's innocent, and you can't visit the sins of the father upon the child" — is both moral greatness and heartbreak. She builds her life inside the fence Troy never finished.
Gatsby — Fitzgerald
Fitzgerald gives his women the least interiority. Daisy is filtered almost entirely through Nick's gaze and Gatsby's obsession. Jordan has more agency but remains a satellite. Myrtle, who has the most vitality, gets the least narrative space and is literally killed. The woman most alive on the page in Gatsby is the one killed first and mourned least — which is itself Fitzgerald's unconscious argument about how the world treats women who dare to want things.
Synthesis
Reading these three texts together exposes how narrative form is itself a moral argument about women. Morrison gives her women full interiority and complex agency. Wilson gives Rose transformative power within a play structurally built around Troy. Fitzgerald largely refuses his women subjectivity. The degree to which each text grants women interiority is a statement about what its culture believed women owed the world — and what the world owed them back. A strong comparative essay can make this formal argument: the most feminist text is also the most formally innovative.
Theme 6
6
Death — Confronted, Avoided, Administered

Death is not background in any of these texts — it is a presence, a character, a philosophical problem. Each text has a completely different relationship to mortality, and those relationships deliver the texts' final moral verdicts.

Sula — Morrison
Death in Sula is intimate and constant. Shadrack institutionalizes it (National Suicide Day). Eva administers it (Plum). Sula witnesses it (Hannah, Chicken Little) without flinching. Sula herself dies slowly and studies the process from inside: "there is just a minute while I'm not breathing but I'm still me." Death in this novel must be domesticated or it destroys you — and Shadrack's final National Suicide Day, when the crowd he leads falls into the flooded tunnel, is what happens when domestication fails.
Fences — Wilson
Troy personifies Death as a wrestling opponent he once bested during a fever. If Death is a person, you can fight it; if it's random, you can't. Troy's actual death comes quietly, doing exercises in the yard — the epic battle he imagined never arrives. The gap between the death he fantasized and the ordinary one he got is the play's quietest tragedy. Gabriel's broken trumpet and his dance are Wilson's answer: the gates of heaven open, but not the way Troy planned.
Gatsby — Fitzgerald
Gatsby's death is grotesque in its smallness: shot by a man with wrong information, floating in a pool, waiting for a call that won't come. No one meaningful attends his funeral. The green light still blinks. Myrtle's death is more violent and more public — her grief (George's) is weaponized by Tom to destroy Gatsby. Both deaths are collateral damage of the careless rich. Fitzgerald uses death to strip the Dream of its glamour: the greatest party-giver of the century dies attended by almost no one.
Synthesis
Each text uses death to deliver its final moral verdict. In Sula, death is always present because Black life in America is always precarious — it must be met directly (Shadrack, Eva) or it undoes you. In Fences, Troy's death reveals the gap between the epic self he imagined and the quiet man he actually was — and Wilson treats this gap with compassion rather than irony. In Gatsby, death exposes the Dream's total indifference to people: the party guests don't come to the funeral; Tom and Daisy don't send flowers. Death in Morrison teaches endurance, in Wilson demands reckoning, in Fitzgerald reveals emptiness.

Sample Exam Prompts & Approaches
All three texts
"In each of these texts, the protagonist is ultimately destroyed by the very quality that made them exceptional. Discuss."
Approach: Sula's radical freedom is what makes her exceptional and what makes the community reject her — she dies without belonging. Troy's enormous will to survive is what made him a great ballplayer and what makes him crush Cory's future — he cannot stop fighting even when the fight is his own son. Gatsby's extraordinary capacity for hope and self-reinvention is what made him great and what killed him — he could not stop believing in a dream the world had already moved past. Thesis: what we call greatness in a person and what destroys them are often the same quality operating in different contexts.
Fences + Gatsby
"Both Troy Maxson and Jay Gatsby feel cheated by America. How do their responses differ, and what does each text suggest about the American Dream?"
Approach: Troy's cheating is racial and real — his exclusion from baseball was systematic. Gatsby's cheating is about class — he cannot buy inherited belonging. Troy responds with hardness and transmission of bitterness to Cory. Gatsby responds with obsessive reconstruction of the past. Both dreams are backward-looking: defined by what was lost or denied, not what's ahead. Thesis: the American Dream is always a dream of recovery, not progress — and both texts show why recovery is impossible when what was lost was never yours to begin with.
Sula + Fences
"In both Sula and Fences, the parent-child relationship is the primary vehicle for exploring how trauma passes between generations. Compare how Morrison and Wilson handle this transmission."
Approach: Morrison's transmission is diffuse and unconscious — Eva doesn't know she's teaching Sula to watch coldly; Hannah doesn't know her admission will wound Sula for life. Wilson's transmission is concrete and visible — Troy directly blocks Cory's scholarship, directly imposing his own wound on his son's future. Morrison shows how damage seeps; Wilson shows how damage strikes. Both suggest the transmission is not inevitable — but both also show how hard it is to interrupt. Strongest argument: ask who has more agency to break the chain, and why neither does.
All three texts
"Across these texts, place is never neutral — it is always a mechanism of power. Discuss how setting enforces or resists social hierarchy."
Approach: The Bottom (founded on a trick, erased for a golf course), the Maxson yard (small Black sovereignty purchased with a brother's disability check), and East/West Egg (class encoded as water) all show that where characters live is determined by forces larger than their choices. Characters who try to cross geographic lines are exposed and diminished by the attempt. Thesis: place enforces hierarchy not through walls but through the naturalization of distance — making the gap between the Valley of Ashes and East Egg, or between the Bottom and the white city below it, feel like geography rather than injustice.
