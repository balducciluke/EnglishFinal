F. Scott Fitzgerald · 1925

The Great Gatsby

Long Island & New York City · Summer 1922

180 pages Scribner's · 1925 Jazz Age / Roaring Twenties
Context & Setting
Time
The Roaring Twenties
Post-WWI America · 1922
Written in 1925, set in the summer of 1922. Post-WWI America is flush with new money, loosened morals, and a giddy, brittle optimism. Prohibition creates speakeasy culture; old money and new money coexist uneasily on Long Island. Fitzgerald saw through the party even while he lived it — underneath the champagne and the green light is profound moral emptiness. The novel is both a celebration of the Jazz Age and its autopsy.
Geography
Four Locations, One Moral Map
Class encoded as space
East Egg = old money, inherited wealth, careless cruelty. West Egg = new money, gaudy ambition, striving. Valley of Ashes = the working poor, the hidden cost of the parties — where George and Myrtle Wilson live and die. New York City = freedom from social rules, where affairs happen and identities dissolve. These four locations form the novel's entire social map, and you can always tell who someone is by where they live.
Characters
Protagonist · ~30 years old · West Egg
Jay Gatsby born James Gatz
New money from bootlegging · Self-invented · Obsessed with Daisy
Gatsby is the American Dream in human form — and its hollow center. Born James Gatz to poor farmers in North Dakota, he reinvented himself entirely: new name, new history, new accent, new mansion. His entire fortune and performance of selfhood exist for one purpose — to reclaim Daisy Buchanan, who represents not a person but a world: the world of old money and effortless belonging that rejected him. Fitzgerald makes Gatsby tragic precisely because his dream is not Daisy herself but the idea of her, which means no real Daisy could ever satisfy it. He is both magnificent and deluded. Nick's judgment — "You're worth the whole damn bunch put together" — is the most complicated compliment in American literature: true, and also insufficient to save him.
"Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!" — Gatsby
Narrator · 29 · Yale · Bond salesman
Nick Carraway
Gatsby's neighbor · Daisy's cousin · Our guide — and our problem
Nick claims to be one of the few honest people he knows — then participates in covering up Myrtle's murder, enables Gatsby's obsession, and judges everyone around him while being morally complicit in their worst actions. He is from the Midwest, educated at Yale, and admires Gatsby with an intensity that borders on romantic. By the end he has seen through the East entirely and retreats home. But the reader should notice: Nick's narration is retrospective, curated, and selective. He is telling us a story he has already shaped. Ask yourself: whose story is he actually telling, and what is he leaving out?
"In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice... 'Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone, just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had.'"
Tom's wife · Gatsby's dream
Daisy Buchanan née Fay
Daisy is the novel's most debated figure. Charming, beautiful, careless — and ultimately she lets Gatsby take the blame for killing Myrtle and leaves with Tom the same afternoon. Is she a villain? Fitzgerald suggests she is something more structural: a product of a world that never required her to be accountable for anything. Her voice, Nick tells us, is "full of money." She doesn't love Gatsby; she loves being loved by him. She chooses safety and privilege over everything, every time, and feels briefly bad about it.
"Her voice is full of money." — Gatsby, as reported by Nick
Daisy's husband · East Egg
Tom Buchanan
The novel's clearest villain. Enormous, athletic, racist (he quotes eugenicist texts with approval), casually unfaithful, and accustomed to absolute entitlement. He destroys Gatsby not because he loves Daisy but because Gatsby challenges his social order. His class allows him to cheat, manipulate, redirect George Wilson's grief toward an innocent man, and face zero consequences. He and Daisy are "careless people" who smash things and retreat into money.
"I suppose the latest thing is to sit back and let Mr. Nobody from Nowhere make love to your wife." — Tom
Tom's mistress · Valley of Ashes
Myrtle Wilson
The novel's most tragic and most overlooked figure. Vital, fleshy, striving — she wants out of the Valley of Ashes and believes Tom is her ticket. She is killed by Daisy's car (driven by Daisy, though Gatsby takes blame) in the most brutal image of class violence in American literature: the rich literally running down the poor and driving away. Fitzgerald gives her almost no interiority. The woman most alive on the page is killed first and mourned least.
Myrtle's husband · Valley of Ashes
George Wilson
Pale, passive, defeated — the Valley of Ashes made human. He works hard, owns a small business, loves a wife who despises him for his poverty. When Myrtle dies, Tom redirects his grief toward Gatsby, knowing Gatsby is innocent. George kills Gatsby then himself. He is used by every powerful person in the novel and discarded. His death completes the pattern: the poor die; the rich move on.
Professional golfer · Nick's love interest
Jordan Baker
Daisy's friend and Nick's entanglement. Jordan is independent, dishonest (she cheated in a tournament), and clear-eyed about the world she inhabits. She represents a version of Daisy who has agency but uses it selfishly. Nick calls her "incurably dishonest" — which is rich, coming from a man covering up a murder. Their failed relationship mirrors the novel's larger argument about whether anyone in this world can truly connect.
Gatsby's associate
Meyer Wolfsheim
The man who fixed the 1919 World Series. His presence signals that Gatsby's money comes from organized crime — the underworld economy that makes self-made men possible. He refuses to attend Gatsby's funeral, one of the novel's bleakest moments. Gatsby built his dream on criminal money and is mourned by almost no one. Wolfsheim's absence is the novel's final measure of how alone Gatsby really was.
Major Symbols
The Green Light
At the end of Daisy's dock across the bay. Gatsby reaches toward it at night. It represents his dream of Daisy — and more broadly, the American Dream itself: always visible, always just across the water, always out of reach. After he meets Daisy again, the green light loses some of its power; the dream is always more powerful than its object. The novel's final image connects it to the original settlers seeing America's "fresh, green breast" — the country that was always a dream, always across some water, always a little bit of a lie.
The Eyes of Doctor T.J. Eckleburg
A faded billboard over the Valley of Ashes: giant blue eyes behind yellow spectacles, staring at everything, presiding over nothing. They represent the eyes of God — or the absence of God: an advertiser's forgotten image now watching over moral wasteland. George Wilson, mad with grief, says "God sees everything" while looking at the billboard. Wilson mistakes commerce for divinity. Fitzgerald's point: in Jazz Age America, the two are indistinguishable. The billboard watches; it judges no one; it sells nothing anymore.
The Valley of Ashes
The industrial wasteland between the Eggs and New York — where the poor live, where Myrtle dies, where George is destroyed. The ashes are literally what remains when the wealthy burn through resources. It is the hidden cost of the parties in West Egg: someone has to live in what those parties produce as waste. Fitzgerald makes the geography moral. You cannot drive from East Egg to New York without passing through the Valley — without passing through the consequence of your own excess.
Gatsby's Parties & The Shirts
The parties are pure performance — Gatsby doesn't drink at them, barely participates, watches the crowd for Daisy. When he throws his shirts at her and she weeps ("I've never seen such beautiful shirts before"), it's the novel's most psychologically acute moment: she's crying because the shirts represent the life she chose and the life she abandoned Gatsby for. The shirts are the American Dream made fabric — beautiful, expensive, and ultimately just cloth. They cannot give Gatsby what he needs, which is not shirts but Daisy's world.
Major Themes
The American Dream as Illusion
Gatsby achieves everything the Dream promises — wealth, reinvention, the great house — and it is structurally hollow. He cannot buy his way into East Egg. He cannot buy the past. The novel's final image — boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past — is Fitzgerald's thesis: America is a country that promises forward motion but is actually structured to prevent it, especially for those without the right birth. The Dream runs backward.
Class & Old Money vs. New
Tom and Daisy's cruelty is enabled by the fact that their money has always been there — they've never had to be accountable to anyone. Gatsby's money is new, criminal, and insufficient to penetrate the world he wants. No matter how many parties he throws, how many shirts he owns, how perfectly he performs, he cannot become East Egg. Old money in this novel is not about wealth; it is about the right to be careless.
Obsession with the Past
Gatsby's fatal flaw is his belief that the past can be repeated — that five years can be erased, that Daisy can be retrieved unchanged, that the dream can be rebuilt from scratch. Nick's line — "Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!" — captures his magnificent delusion. Gatsby lives entirely in an imagined past that was probably never as perfect as he remembers it. He is destroyed by temporal thinking: the inability to accept that time moves forward only.
Carelessness as Moral Corruption
Fitzgerald's indictment of the wealthy is concentrated in one word: careless. Tom and Daisy are "careless people" — they break things and people and retreat into money. No one is held accountable for Myrtle's death. No one meaningful attends Gatsby's funeral. Wealth in this novel is not glamorous — it is a mechanism for escaping consequence. The Buchanans are not villains in the dramatic sense. They are simply people who were never required to care.
Identity & Self-Invention
Gatsby/James Gatz is America's purest expression of self-invention. He rewrites himself from poverty to glamour, creates a biography, changes his name. But his invented self is entirely contingent on Daisy's acceptance — without her validation, James Gatz has no Jay Gatsby. When Tom strips away the facade ("Mr. Nobody from Nowhere"), the construction collapses. The novel asks: can you truly reinvent yourself, or does the original self always remain, waiting?
Nick's Reliability & Complicity
A sophisticated essay will note that Nick is not a neutral narrator. He enables Gatsby's obsession, covers up Daisy's role in Myrtle's death, participates in the affair logistics, then judges everyone from a position of apparent innocence. His narration is retrospective and curated. He is not the honest man he claims to be. The most interesting reading of the novel is one where Nick's infatuation with Gatsby and with a world he claims to reject is itself the subject.

Exam Writing Tips
If your prompt is about the American Dream
Don't just say "Gatsby represents the American Dream." Argue that Fitzgerald uses Gatsby to expose the Dream as structurally deceptive — it promises meritocracy but delivers class rigidity. New money cannot become old money no matter how much accumulates. Use East vs. West Egg, Gatsby's criminal fortune, and his social rejection despite his wealth. The green light is your best symbol: always visible, always across the water, always out of reach.
If your prompt is about identity or self-invention
Gatsby's reinvention is brilliant and fatal: his invented self is contingent on Daisy's validation, which means it was never truly his own. Compare to Troy in Fences (built himself around baseball — when that's gone, he has no blueprint) and Sula (builds a self with no external validation at all, which grants real freedom but no community). All three texts show that self-invention is possible but precarious, and that what you build yourself around can become what destroys you.
If your prompt is about Nick as narrator
Argue that Nick is not trustworthy. He claims honesty; he enables a cover-up. He claims distance; he is infatuated with Gatsby. He claims to judge the careless rich; he participates in their world. The most interesting version of this essay asks: why does Nick tell this story the way he does? What is he protecting? What does his curation of events reveal about his own complicity in Gatsby's destruction?
